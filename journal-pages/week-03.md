## Independent Learning

### Process
*To begin my live data experiment, I brainstormed a range of possible directions for the project. I sketched out ideas for how different live data sources could be translated into physical form. From that brainstorm, I landed on the Weather Spine concept, a growing, vertebrae-like sculpture built from cardboard or paper, where each day's observation adds a new unit to the structure. Once the concept was defined, I trialled the project over three days, checking the weather each morning and translating the conditions — temperature, wind, and sky — into a physical cardboard unit following my own rules. Each unit was folded, scored, or torn according to the day's weather, then added to the growing spine. Throughout the process, I made sure to document my work at each stage, recording both the data I was observing and the physical changes to the sculpture as it accumulated, so that the progression from day one to day three was clearly traceable.*

### Did you take a digital or analogue/physical approach? Why?
*I chose the analogue/physical approach because I wanted to work with something I could actually touch and hold over time. There's something about a screen-based visualisation that feels immediate but forgettable, and I am not as proficient with coding as I am with physical making. Physical is my preferred medium because you can look at it, absorb the information, and move on. The idea of building something day by day, where each morning's observation literally adds mass to a structure, felt like it matched the nature of weather itself: slow, incremental, only meaningful when you zoom out.*

### What live data source did you work with, and how did you access it?
*I worked with daily weather observations, specifically temperature, wind, and sky conditions. I took inspiration from our in-class activities on the weather visualisation. For the 3 days that I completed the experiment to gain an understanding of my project, I checked the weather app on my phone each morning. I also made sure to go outside and feel my surroundings so I could understand what I was visualising. There's a difference between knowing it's 12 degrees and feeling that it's cold and still. That experiential quality felt important to preserve in the protocol.*

### How did you decide on the mapping between data and material form?
*I made the mappings feel intuitive rather than random. Temperature controls width - warmer days produce wider units - because heat expansion is a real physical phenomenon, so the analogy has some logic to it. Wind controls the angle of the fold because wind is about deflection and tilt. Rain is marked by tearing rather than cutting - rain is disruptive, irregular, and it resists clean edges. I wanted someone picking up the sculpture to make educated guesses about what the encodings meant before reading the key, which is a sign that the mappings are working.*

### What does your work reveal or communicate about the data?
*The thing that surprised me most was how the vertebrae of the spine actually looked very different from day to day. If I had spent more time with higher-quality materials, the look would have fluctuated more. Still, as for what I achieved with this prototype physical artefact assignment, I am happy with how it turned out.*

### Did you use vibe coding, LLMs, or other tools in your process? What did you learn?
*I used an LLM (Claude) in the early ideation stage to help develop and stress-test the concept. I described what I was trying to achieve and used that to assess whether the protocol was clear enough for someone else to follow, and to refine the mapping logic. What I learned from that process is that expressing your ideas in a way an AI can understand is actually a useful design exercise in itself. The LLM didn't make creative decisions for me, but it helped me identify gaps in my own thinking faster than I would have on my own.*

### How does your work relate to the practitioner examples discussed in class?
*The clearest connection is to Sol LeWitt; the protocol is the work. I could hand someone my instruction sheet, and they would produce a legitimate version of the piece, which was intentional. Conditional Design is present in how the rules interact: it's not just temperature or wind, it's both at once, producing a single unit, and those combinations create unexpected results you couldn't predict in advance. Nathalie Miebach is probably the most direct material influence; her work demonstrates that data encoded in three-dimensional form can be read spatially and physically in ways that flat visualisation can't replicate, and that's exactly what I was testing with the spine.*

### What would you develop further with more time?
*I'd want to run the protocol across a full season rather than a week or two, so the sculpture becomes genuinely monumental, long enough to drape or coil. I'd also like to have multiple people run the protocol simultaneously in different areas to see differences in the sculpture. The comparison between a Wellington spine and an Auckland spine over the same period would say something about place and climate (Windy Welly). I'd also experiment with different materials. Maybe denim, wire, or paper with different thicknesses.*

### Other Reflections?
*Checking the weather at the same time each morning, standing outside, making the unit, it became a practice for those 3 days. I was more attentive to the weather than I normally am.*
